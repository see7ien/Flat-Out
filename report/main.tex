\title{Full-Throttle Speed Loss in 2026 Formula One Qualifying:\\
A Descriptive Measurement from Public Telemetry}
\author{Ang See Tien\\National University of Singapore}
\date{October 2026}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
The 2026 Formula One technical regulations nearly triple the power of the kinetic motor generator unit (MGU-K), to 350~kW, and reduce that power with speed above 290~km/h. One visible consequence is \emph{clipping}: cars lose speed on straights while the driver is still at full throttle. We measure this effect in every 2026 qualifying session held up to the Singapore Grand Prix, using public FastF1 telemetry checked against OpenF1 and cleaned with documented sentinel values only. Across 41 reportable straights in 16 sessions (842 driver-straights), 86\% of driver-straights reach their peak speed before the end of the full-throttle segment. Differences between power-unit manufacturers are small: the five median values span 2.1~km/h, about the same as the median gap between teammates (2.0~km/h). On 39 straights matched between 2025 and 2026 at 15 circuits, all 33 straights that show clipping have an earlier peak and a larger speed loss in 2026; the other 6 show no clipping in either year. The analysis is descriptive and makes no causal claims.
\end{abstract}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Introduction}

The 2026 Formula One regulations moved the sport much further towards electrical power. The MGU-K, the motor-generator on the drivetrain, may now deliver 350~kW instead of 120~kW, while the heat-recovery unit on the turbocharger has been removed \citep{fia2026c, medland2026rules}. The battery therefore has to be recharged far more often from the car's own kinetic energy, and the regulations ration both how much energy may be recovered on each lap and how much electrical power may be used at high speed. From pre-season testing onwards, drivers and commentators described a new behaviour: cars that stop accelerating, or visibly slow, before the braking point on a straight, with the driver still flat out \citep{noble2026superclipping, noble2026leclerc, f1technical2026}.

That behaviour matters because speed lost at full throttle is lap time that cannot be recovered on that straight. It also raised sporting and safety concerns. After the first three rounds, the FIA, teams and power-unit (PU) manufacturers agreed changes to the energy rules for qualifying, which applied from the Miami Grand Prix \citep{fia2026refinements}. Yet the public evidence for the effect remains anecdotal: single laps, single corners, and broadcast speed traces \citep{noble2026leclerc, f1technical2026}. To our knowledge, nobody has yet measured the effect systematically across a season from public data.

Energy-limited deployment has a well-developed theory. Optimal-control studies of the 2014--2025 hybrid power unit show that, under a fixed energy budget per lap, the lap-time-optimal strategy spends electrical energy unevenly. It is concentrated where it saves the most time, so some parts of the lap are deliberately run with less electrical support \citep{limebeer2014ers, salazar2017policy, ebbesen2018timeoptimal}. Those studies use team-grade models and data. Work from public data is rarer. \citet{bopaiah2020strategy} reconstructed energy-recovery use from on-board broadcast graphics. \citet{bopaiah2021} compared drivers, teams sharing a PU, and the whole field in a single qualifying session, using positive kinetic energy as a proxy for deployment. Statistical studies of Formula One performance have so far worked from race results rather than telemetry \citep{bell2016formula, vankesteren2023bayesian}.

This paper measures full-throttle speed loss across the 2026 season from public telemetry. It follows the validation approach of \citet{bopaiah2021}, checking reconstructed lap distance against the official track length, and extends it in three directions. Every lap and segment it excludes is logged with a reason, so each result can be traced back to the raw data. It reports continuous measurements in physical units, rather than normalised modes. And it covers a whole season, with a matched set of 2025 sessions for comparison, rather than one session. We ask three descriptive questions:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Where on a straight do 2026 cars reach their peak speed, and how much speed do they lose after it?
  \item How large are the differences between PU manufacturers compared with the differences between teammates?
  \item How do the same straights compare between 2025 and 2026?
\end{enumerate}
Section~\ref{sec:season} answers the first two from the 2026 season alone, and Section~\ref{sec:years} answers the third.

\section{Background}

\subsection{From the 2014 hybrid power unit to 2026}

From 2014 to 2025, the Formula One power unit combined a 1.6-litre turbocharged engine with two motor-generators. The MGU-K, on the drivetrain, recovered energy under braking and returned it as propulsion. The MGU-H, on the turbocharger, recovered energy from the exhaust. The MGU-K was limited to 120~kW and could recover at most 2~MJ per lap, but energy through the MGU-H was unrestricted, so a car could deploy up to 4~MJ per lap from its energy store \citep{bopaiah2020strategy, bopaiah2021, limebeer2014ers}.

The 2026 regulations remove the MGU-H. Only the engine and the kinetic energy recovery system (ERS-K) may now propel the car or recover energy (Article C5.2.1), and the ERS-K's electrical power rises to 350~kW (C5.2.7) \citep{fia2026c}.\footnote{Article numbers and values refer to Issue 20 of Section C (5 August 2026). Compared with Issue 12 (June 2025), every value used here is unchanged. Two modes have been renamed (Override Mode is now Overtake, and Straight-Line Mode is now Straight Mode), and the event-level reductions of the recovery limit in C5.2.10 now reach 7~MJ, and 4~MJ in qualifying.} The engine now supplies only about half of the total power \citep{medland2026rules}, and the energy the ERS-K may recover rises to 8.5~MJ per lap (C5.2.10), more than four times the previous MGU-K limit. Because that energy can now come only from the MGU-K, the car has to harvest it from its own motion: under braking, on partial throttle, or by running the MGU-K as a generator against the engine. The cars are also lighter and smaller. DRS is gone: Overtake, a mode with extra electrical power at high speed (C5.2.8\,ii), replaces it as the overtaking aid, and the front and rear wings now switch between a ``Corner Mode'' and a lower-drag ``Straight Mode'' (C3.10, C3.11) \citep{fia2026c, medland2026rules}. Table~\ref{tab:rules} summarises the changes that bear on this study.

\subsection{Power reduction at high speed}

The 350~kW limit applies only at lower speeds. Article C5.2.8\,i limits the MGU-K power used to propel the car to $P = 1800 - 5v$~kW for $v < 340$~km/h, to $6900 - 20v$~kW for $340 \le v < 345$~km/h, and to zero at 345~km/h and above \citep{fia2026c}. Combined with the 350~kW cap, the car has full electrical power up to
\begin{equation}
  v_\text{onset} = \frac{1800 - 350}{5} = 290~\text{km/h}.
\end{equation}
Above this speed, the permitted power falls by 5~kW for every additional km/h, to 100~kW at 340~km/h and zero at 345~km/h (Figure~\ref{fig:taper}). Overtake, whose use is governed by the Sporting Regulations, shifts the curve to higher speeds (C5.2.8\,ii). Many 2026 straights cross the onset. At Suzuka, for example, peak speeds on the three straights measured here ranged from 281 to 338~km/h: the T12$\to$T13 straight peaks around the onset, and the other two well above it.

\subsection{Clipping and superclipping}

On a straight, a car accelerates while its propulsive power exceeds the power needed to overcome aerodynamic drag and rolling resistance. Drag force grows with the square of speed, so the power it absorbs grows with the cube \citep{milliken1995racecar}. When propulsive power falls below that requirement, the car slows down even if the driver keeps the throttle fully open. In 2026, three mechanisms can cause this. The electrical power can be reduced by the speed-dependent limit above. The car can run out of energy that it is permitted, or has chosen, to deploy on that straight. Or the MGU-K can be switched into generator mode, taking power from the engine to recharge the battery. Commentators call the second \emph{clipping}, a term that predates 2026, and the third \emph{superclipping} \citep{mitchellmalm2026weird, noble2026superclipping, racer2026clipping}. Superclipping is typically used at the end of straights, where recovering energy costs the least lap time, and its peak power was capped by the regulations \citep{noble2026superclipping, fia2026refinements}.

Public telemetry cannot tell these mechanisms apart. The car-data stream carries six channels (Speed, RPM, gear, Throttle, Brake and DRS) at roughly 4~Hz \citep{fastf1, openf1}. It includes no ERS channel: no MGU-K power or torque, no energy store state of charge, and no indication of whether the MGU-K is deploying or harvesting. In this paper, \emph{clipping} therefore refers to the observable outcome, speed falling within a full-throttle segment, whatever its cause.

\subsection{In-season changes and the Miami split}

The first rounds made the cost of the new rules visible. Drivers complained that qualifying laps had become exercises in energy management, with part-throttle running and long superclipping phases. In one case a car lost up to 15~km/h for much of a straight compared with the same driver's previous run \citep{noble2026leclerc}. The FIA first lowered the maximum energy that could be recharged in qualifying, from 9 to 8~MJ, for the Japanese Grand Prix \citep{f1technical2026suzuka}. On 20 April the FIA, teams, PU manufacturers and Formula One Management agreed a wider package, which took effect from the Miami Grand Prix \citep{fia2026refinements}. For qualifying, the maximum recharge fell from 8 to 7~MJ, with the stated aim of limiting superclipping to roughly two to four seconds per lap, and peak superclipping power rose from 250 to 350~kW. Lower energy limits could also now be applied at 12 events instead of 8.

These changes act directly on the quantity measured here. Less energy to recover should shorten superclipping phases. A higher harvesting power makes each phase shorter but sharper. We therefore split the season into a pre-Miami period (Australia, China and Japan; 3 sessions) and a post-Miami period (14 sessions). The two periods also differ in data quality (Section~\ref{sec:freeze}), and no circuit appears in both, so we report the split but do not attribute the difference between the periods to the rule changes (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\section{Data and measurement}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Sources and sessions}

Telemetry comes from FastF1 version~3.8.3 \citep{fastf1}, an open-source Python package that serves the official Formula One live-timing feed: car data (Speed, RPM, gear, Throttle, Brake) and position data (X/Y). FastF1 has been used in the same way in recent peer-reviewed work \citep{cappello2026tire}. Brake values are cross-checked against the \var{car\_data} endpoint of OpenF1 \citep{openf1}, which was available for every session used.

The 2026 calendar was announced with 24 rounds. The Bahrain and Saudi Arabian Grands Prix, scheduled for April, were cancelled in March because of the conflict in the Middle East, which left 22 rounds \citep{sky2026cancel}. The Bahrain Grand Prix was later restaged at the Sepang circuit in Malaysia in October, so the season now has 23 rounds \citep{f12026malaysia}. Seventeen qualifying sessions had been held by the Singapore Grand Prix (10 October 2026), and all are included. FastF1 has no corner positions yet for the new Madrid circuit or for Sepang, which last hosted Formula One in 2017 \citep{fastf1issue692}, so straights there cannot be labelled by the corner they end at. Sepang therefore contributes only its main straight, and Madrid no reportable straight. For 2025, we use the 15 qualifying sessions held at circuits that were also raced in 2026, matched by OpenF1 \var{circuit\_key}.

\subsection{Validation of distance}

Following \citet{bopaiah2021}, we checked each driver's fastest-lap distance at Suzuka 2026 against the official track length of 5.807~km. Distance integrated from X/Y position was within $-0.83$ to $-0.43$\% (mean absolute error 0.58\%). Distance integrated from car-data speed had a mean absolute error of 1.48\% and a maximum of 7.37\%. For comparison, \citet{bopaiah2021} report a mean error below 0.6\% and a maximum below 1.8\% for their reconstructed data. The larger car-data errors come from frozen samples, described next.

\subsection{Frozen samples}
\label{sec:freeze}

Car data sometimes freeze: Speed, RPM and gear stay constant while the car keeps moving. On the 2026 Suzuka pole lap, car data froze at 189~km/h for 25.8~s. The same freezes appear in OpenF1, so they originate upstream of FastF1. Figure~\ref{fig:freeze} shows how unevenly they are distributed. In 2025, the share of frozen samples on push laps never exceeded 0.06\% at any paired circuit. In 2026 it reached 10.6\%, with Shanghai, Suzuka and Monza above 1\%. Averaged by period, the freeze share was 7.0\% before Miami and 0.2\% from Miami onwards. Any segment that overlaps a freeze is dropped. Freezes are detected only from values that FastF1 documents, with no tuned thresholds; the rules are given in full in Appendix~\ref{app:cleaning}.

\subsection{Push laps and full-throttle segments}

\emph{Push laps} are laps that do not involve the pit lane, are not deleted by the stewards, and are within 107\% of the session's fastest lap time. The 107\% threshold is FastF1's default for identifying representative laps \citep{fastf1}, borrowed from the qualifying cut-off in the Sporting Regulations \citep{fia2026sporting}. It removes out-laps, cool-down laps and aborted laps, which would otherwise be mistaken for clipping. We also reject laps whose speed is zero throughout, whose X/Y position is $(0,0)$ on any sample, or whose position does not change at all.

A \emph{full-throttle segment} is a run of consecutive samples with the throttle at 98\% or more and the brake off (or unknown), at least 500~m long. FastF1 records Throttle as pedal position from 0 to 100\% \citep{fastf1}. A threshold slightly below 100 keeps a straight intact when the reading dips marginally below full pedal. For the same reason, a single sample below the threshold does not end a segment. At about 4~Hz, one sample spans roughly 20~m at 300~km/h, so a one-sample dip is far too short to be a deliberate lift. The 500~m minimum (about 25 samples at that speed) keeps genuine straights and excludes short bursts of throttle between corners, where the car is still accelerating and the position of the peak carries little information. Neither choice drives the conclusions. Across throttle thresholds of 95, 98 and 100\% and minimum lengths of 300, 500 and 800~m (Appendix~\ref{app:sens}), most driver-straights peak before the segment end and the median paired 2025--2026 difference is positive in all nine combinations. The only exception to the straight-by-straight count is Silverstone T16, a segment of about 310~m that qualifies only at the 300~m minimum and whose median loss is below 1~km/h in both years. The throttle threshold barely changes the results; the minimum length changes their size, because longer minimums keep only long straights, which peak earlier and lose more speed. This is the same confounding with straight length discussed in Section~\ref{sec:res-peak}.

Each segment is labelled by the corner it ends at, matched by X/Y position to FastF1's corner list (Figure~\ref{fig:map}). The main straight is stitched across the timing line and reported separately. For each driver and straight we keep one lap: from the latest qualifying part in which the driver has a clean segment on that straight, the fastest such lap. A straight is \emph{reportable} in a session only if at least 10 drivers have a clean segment on it. This gives 41 reportable straights in 16 sessions (842 driver-straights): 8 straights before Miami and 33 from Miami onwards.

\subsection{Segment metrics}

For each selected segment, with distance $d$ from its start and length $L$, we record four quantities.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \var{peak\_speed}: the maximum speed in the segment. If the maximum is reached on several samples, we take the last of them.
  \item \var{vmax\_position} $= d_\text{peak}/L$: where the peak occurs, from 0 (segment start) to 1 (segment end). A value below 1 means the car reached its top speed before the driver lifted or braked.
  \item \var{clip\_delta} $=$ \var{peak\_speed} $-$ \var{end\_speed}: the \emph{speed lost after the peak} (km/h), measured up to the last full-throttle sample.
  \item \var{decel\_rate} $= 100\,\cdot$ \var{clip\_delta}$/(L - d_\text{peak})$: the speed lost per 100~m after the peak.
\end{itemize}
Speed is reported in whole km/h, so a car running at a constant, drag-limited top speed can register a peak slightly before the segment end with no real loss. We therefore also report the share of drivers with \var{clip\_delta}~$\ge 2$~km/h.

All comparisons in this paper are descriptive. There is one lap per driver, and straights at the same circuit are not independent of one another, so formal significance tests would overstate the evidence. We report counts, medians and interquartile ranges (IQR) instead.

\section{The 2026 season}
\label{sec:season}

The results in this section use all 842 driver-straights. The first subsection uses the raw values. For the comparison between PU manufacturers, each straight's mean over all its clean drivers is first subtracted from each driver's value (\emph{centring}). This removes everything common to all cars on a straight, such as its length, gradient and the energy limit set for that event, so that what remains reflects differences between cars and drivers. Centred values are then pooled by PU.

\subsection{Most 2026 cars peak before the end of the straight}
\label{sec:res-peak}

In 2026 qualifying, most cars reach their peak speed before the end of the full-throttle segment and then lose speed while still at full throttle. Figure~\ref{fig:vmax} shows where the peak falls. If cars accelerated all the way to the braking point, the histogram would be a single bar at 1. Instead, 86\% of the 842 driver-straights peak before the segment end, and the median peak position is 0.76, about three-quarters of the way along the straight. This matches descriptions of 2026 cars peaking earlier on a straight than earlier cars \citep{therace2026fix}, but the 2026 data alone cannot test that comparison; Section~\ref{sec:years} makes it with 2025 data.

How much speed is lost after the peak depends on how fast the car is going, but not in a simple way (Figure~\ref{fig:scatter}). Each point is one driver-straight, and the black line follows the median in 5~km/h bins of peak speed. The median loss is \emph{higher} below the 290~km/h onset than above it: 13.0~km/h for the 106 driver-straights that peak below it, against 11.0~km/h for the 736 that peak at or above it. Below the onset, the speed-dependent limit does not restrict power. Four of the five straights whose median peak is below the onset contain at least one corner taken flat out (Shanghai T8, Monaco T10, Spa-Francorchamps T12 and Baku T15), where cars lose speed through drag and cornering load whatever the power available; the fifth, the Montreal main straight, contains none. Above the onset, the loss rises with peak speed (Spearman $\rho = 0.36$; \citealp{spearman1904}). This is consistent with the speed-dependent limit, but it does not show that the limit causes the loss. Peak speed is confounded with straight length: longer straights allow higher peak speeds and also leave more distance over which to lose speed. Circuit is confounded with both.

\subsection{PU differences are about as large as teammate differences}
\label{sec:res-pu}

Teammates share a car and a power unit, so the gap between them gives a natural yardstick for differences that come from driving and from lap-to-lap variation rather than from the car. Results-level studies use the same logic to separate driver from constructor \citep{bell2016formula, vankesteren2023bayesian}. Once each straight's mean is removed, the spread between PU manufacturers is about the same as the gap between teammates. Figure~\ref{fig:pu} shows the centred values by PU. The PU medians of centred \var{clip\_delta} range from $-0.9$~km/h (Mercedes) to $+1.1$~km/h (Honda), a span of 2.1~km/h, while teammates on the same straight differ by a median 2.0~km/h (IQR 1.0--4.0, 408 pairs). Within a single PU, team medians span up to 3.8~km/h. The PU medians of centred \var{decel\_rate} range from $-0.49$ to $0.29$~km/h per 100~m.

Splitting by speed regime gives a similar picture. Here each straight is assigned as a whole by its median peak speed, so the counts differ slightly from the per-driver split in Section~\ref{sec:res-peak}. On the 5 straights with a median peak below 290~km/h (88 driver-straights), PU medians range from $-0.4$ to $2.9$~km/h. On the 36 straights at or above 290~km/h (754 driver-straights), they range from $-0.9$ to $2.4$~km/h. With one or two cars per team and one lap per driver, the sample is too small to rank the manufacturers.

\section{Comparison with 2025}
\label{sec:years}

Comparing across years needs straights that exist in both. A straight is \emph{matched} if the same corner label exists at the same circuit in both years, with at least 10 clean drivers in each. This yields 39 matched straights at 15 circuits. A matched straight \emph{shows no clipping} if its median \var{clip\_delta} is zero in both years, and counts as a straight \emph{with clipping} otherwise.

Each straight is compared with itself. For each matched straight and year we take the median over drivers (one lap each). We then take the 2026~$-$~2025 difference per straight, and report the median and IQR of those differences. Pairing in this way holds the circuit, the corner and the length of the straight constant. All 2026 cars are pooled, because Section~\ref{sec:res-pu} found the differences between PUs to be small.

Two checks guard against artefacts. First, a changed braking point or a mislabelled corner would move the end of a segment. We therefore flag any straight whose median segment end point moved more than 50~m between years, using position data. Second, rain lowers grip and speeds, changes lines and tyre choice, and alters where a driver can stay flat out \citep{pirelli2020wet}. From Miami onwards, wet sessions also run with reduced maximum ERS deployment \citep{fia2026refinements}, and the regulations treat wet qualifying differently, for instance by suspending the 107\% rule \citep{fia2026sporting}. A wet session could therefore change the measured loss for reasons unrelated to the year. We repeat the comparison without any circuit at which FastF1 weather data record rainfall at any point in qualifying in either year.

\subsection{Every straight that clips does so earlier and harder in 2026}
\label{sec:res-years}

On all 33 matched straights with clipping, 2026 cars peak earlier and lose more speed after the peak than 2025 cars did on the same straight. The other 6 matched straights show no clipping in either year.

\paragraph{Speed signature.} Figure~\ref{fig:signature} pools all matched straights into a single speed profile per year. Each driver's segment is stretched to a common length and its speed divided by its own peak, so straights of different lengths and speeds can be laid on top of one another. In 2025, the median curve climbs all the way to the end of the segment: the median peak position over driver-straights is 1.00. In 2026, it rises faster, peaks at a median 0.77 of the way along, and fades before the braking point. Table~\ref{tab:paired} gives 0.99 for 2025 instead, because it is the median of the per-straight medians. The 2026 curve lies above the 2025 curve from the start of the segment to 0.84 of its length, with a gap of 0.025 of peak speed at the start, which indicates a faster early gain relative to peak speed. The curves then cross: the 2026 curve ends at 96.4\% of peak speed, against 100.0\% in 2025.

\paragraph{Straight by straight.} The pooled profile could in principle be driven by a few straights. Figure~\ref{fig:dumbbell} shows that it is not: each row is one matched straight, with 2025 and 2026 joined by a line, as recommended for paired data \citep{weissgerber2015}. On every clipped straight, the 2026 point sits to the left in the peak-position panel and to the right in the speed-loss panel. Across the 33, the peak moved towards the start by a median 0.20 of the straight (range 0.01 to 0.59), and speed lost after the peak grew by a median 10.5~km/h (range 1.0 to 50.5). The smallest changes count the same as the largest: Monza T4 ($-0.01$, $+2.0$~km/h) and the Baku main straight ($-0.01$, $+1.0$~km/h). Some segments include corners taken flat out, where speed falls even in 2025 because of drag and cornering load; the pairing compares each such straight with itself. Table~\ref{tab:paired} summarises the paired differences across all 39 straights.

\section{Discussion}

Taken together, the three findings describe a behaviour that is widespread, new and shared across the field. Most 2026 driver-straights peak before the end of the full-throttle segment. Above the 290~km/h onset, the speed lost after the peak tends to grow with peak speed, although peak speed is confounded with straight length. The spread between PU manufacturers is no larger than the gap between teammates, so at this resolution the data do not separate the manufacturers. Against 2025, every matched straight that clips does so earlier and harder. The pattern is therefore better described as a property of the 2026 cars as a group than of any one manufacturer, although one or two cars per team cannot rule out smaller PU differences.

The shape of the 2026 speed profile fits what optimal-control studies predict for an energy-limited hybrid. When the energy per lap is capped, the time-optimal strategy spends it where it saves the most time and accepts less support elsewhere \citep{salazar2017policy, ebbesen2018timeoptimal}. A faster early rise followed by a fade before braking is what one would expect if electrical power is concentrated on acceleration and withdrawn, or reversed into recovery, near the end of the straight, where speed and drag are highest. The descriptive data are consistent with that explanation but cannot confirm it.

Compared with \citet{bopaiah2021}, this approach trades depth for breadth. They studied one qualifying session in detail. Here a single observable is measured across a season, against a matched set of 2025 sessions, with every exclusion logged and with cleaning rules developed on one event (Suzuka) and then applied unchanged to the rest. The two approaches complement each other: a detailed single-session study can show how a car uses its energy, and a season-wide measurement shows how general a pattern is.

The causal question, how much of the loss comes from the speed-dependent power limit and how much from energy limits or from team and driver choices, cannot be answered from public telemetry. It would require the energy store's state of charge, the MGU-K's deployment and harvesting power along the lap, the energy recovered per lap against the event's limit, and the use of Overtake. None of these is in the public feed. With those channels, taper-limited, energy-limited and harvest-driven speed loss could be separated straight by straight, and a model adjusting for straight length could replace the descriptive comparisons used here.

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limits}

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Descriptive design.} We report counts, medians and ranges, not estimated effects. Interval estimates that resample by circuit, and a model that adjusts for straight length, are natural next steps.
  \item \textbf{Confounding.} Peak speed is confounded with straight length and circuit (Section~\ref{sec:res-peak}). Energy limits are also set event by event, so part of the variation between circuits may reflect the limit in force rather than the circuit itself \citep{fia2026refinements}.
  \item \textbf{The Miami split.} No circuit appears in both periods, and pre-Miami data quality was worse (mean freeze share 7.0\% against 0.2\%). Median \var{clip\_delta} was 26.0~km/h on the 8 pre-Miami straights and 9.0~km/h on the 33 post-Miami straights. The direction is consistent with the lower qualifying recharge limit introduced at Miami, but the difference cannot be separated from circuit or data quality, so we do not interpret it.
  \item \textbf{Whole-package comparison.} The 2025--2026 difference reflects every change between the two cars, including the aerodynamic modes and the lower mass, not the power-unit rules alone.
  \item \textbf{No ERS channels.} Public telemetry has no state of charge, deployment or harvesting power, so a given speed loss cannot be attributed to the speed-dependent limit, to energy limits, to superclipping, or to driver choice.
  \item \textbf{Sample.} There are one or two cars per team and one lap per driver per straight. Madrid and Sepang have no corner-labelled straights.
  \item \textbf{Measurement.} Speed is reported in whole km/h, and the reported magnitudes depend on the minimum segment length (Appendix~\ref{app:sens}). Freezes remove more data in some sessions (up to 10.6\% at Suzuka 2026). The \var{JumpFlag} check (Appendix~\ref{app:cleaning}) was run for Suzuka only, where 1 of 62 selected segments was flagged and excluding it changed the mean \var{clip\_delta} per straight by at most 0.4~km/h. Its bound is derived from 2026 values and is not interpreted for 2025.
\end{itemize}

% ---------------------------------------------------------------------------
\section{Conclusion}

Public telemetry, cleaned with documented sentinel values only, is enough to measure full-throttle speed loss across a Formula One season. In 2026 qualifying, losing speed before the end of a straight is the norm rather than the exception. It is not specific to one manufacturer, and on the same straights it is new relative to 2025. Explaining why it happens is a separate task: separating the speed-dependent power limit from energy limits and superclipping requires the energy-system channels that teams hold but the public feed does not carry.

\section{Cleaning rules}
\label{app:cleaning}

Freezes and unknown brake states are identified from documented sentinel values only, with no thresholds tuned to the data. The one rule with a numerical bound, (c), flags segments but removes none:
\begin{itemize}
  \item[(a)] A run of unchanged Speed, RPM and gear that contains \var{Throttle}~$=104$ is a freeze, plus a one-sample buffer on each side. FastF1 documents 104 as an error or unavailable value, usually seen when the car is stationary \citep{fastf1}, and its maintainer notes that it should not occur in lap telemetry \citep{fastf1issue772}. We observe it while cars are running. Segments that overlap a freeze are dropped.
  \item[(b)] OpenF1 \var{brake}~$=104$ means the brake state is unknown. It does not end a full-throttle segment and is trimmed from the segment's end. OpenF1 \var{brake}~$=100$ is taken as genuine braking.
  \item[(c)] A single-step speed increase of at least 5~km/h beyond what 800~kW could produce for a 700~kg car is flagged (\var{JumpFlag}), not removed. Results are reported with and without flagged segments, in the spirit of a multiverse analysis \citep{steegen2016}.
\end{itemize}
We also tested a Speed/RPM ratio check \citep[sec.~6.4]{segers2014}. It did not separate stale from updating speed, so it is used only as a diagnostic. Figure~\ref{fig:freezemap} shows where the flagged samples fall on the Suzuka lap in 2026.
